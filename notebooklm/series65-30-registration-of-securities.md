# Registration of Securities

This is one topic from the Series 65 exam, the Uniform Investment Adviser Law
Examination. It is worth about 2 percent of the exam.

## Why this topic matters

Unless a security is federal covered or otherwise exempt, it must be registered in each state where it is offered. State law provides three methods — notification, coordination, and qualification — that differ in who may use them and when effectiveness occurs. This topic also covers what NSMIA preempted, what notice-filing power the states kept, and the limits on the Administrator's authority.

## First Question: Is It a Security?

Registration rules only matter if the instrument is a security in the first place, and the drafters faced an obvious problem. Anyone who wanted to sell an investment free of the securities laws could simply avoid calling it stock. So the statutory definition lists the familiar instruments — notes, stocks, bonds, debentures, certificates of interest in a profit-sharing agreement, voting-trust certificates, and so on — and then adds the open-ended catch-all: **investment contract**. That phrase is what keeps the definition from being defeated by relabeling.

### The Howey Test

**SEC v. W.J. Howey Co.** gave the test. An **investment contract** exists where there is:

1. An **investment of money** (or other consideration),
2. in a **common enterprise**,
3. with an **expectation of profits**,
4. derived **primarily from the efforts of others**.

All four elements must be present. The fourth is where most fact patterns are decided, because it captures the underlying policy: when an investor is dependent on someone else's management for the return, the investor cannot protect himself by inspection or effort, and disclosure is the only protection available. When the investor is doing the work, he can see for himself.

Howey itself involved orange groves. The defendant sold small strips of a Florida citrus grove together with a service contract under which the defendant cultivated, harvested, and marketed the fruit and remitted the proceeds. Buyers were mostly out-of-state professionals who never set foot in the grove. Real estate plus a service contract is not on anyone's list of securities — but the buyers put in money, pooled it in one operation, expected profits, and did nothing themselves. It was an investment contract.

### Howey Applied to Non-Obvious Instruments

- **Whiskey and commodity storage programs, ostrich and cattle-breeding programs, equipment leasing partnerships.** Where the promoter manages the asset and remits the profit, these are securities regardless of the underlying thing.
- **Condominium units sold with a mandatory rental pool.** A condominium by itself is real estate. Add an arrangement in which the developer rents the unit, pools the revenue with other units, and pays the owner a share, and the four elements are satisfied.
- **General versus limited partnership interests.** A **limited partnership interest is almost always a security**: the limited partner contributes capital and by definition may not manage. A **general partnership interest usually is not**, because the general partner has management rights and can protect himself. But the labels do not control — if a "general" partner is passive, geographically remote, and has no realistic ability to exercise the powers on paper, courts have found a security.
- **Promissory notes.** Notes are presumed to be securities, with familiar commercial exceptions carved out — consumer and home-mortgage notes, short-term notes secured by a lien on a small business, and notes evidencing an ordinary loan from a bank.
- **Not securities:** fixed annuities and whole life insurance (the insurer bears the investment risk), commodity futures and forward contracts themselves, collectibles, currency, and a straightforward interest in real estate with no management arrangement attached.

**Variable annuities and variable life are securities** precisely because the Howey logic bites: the contract owner puts money into a separate account, bears the investment risk, and depends on the insurer's portfolio management for the return.

## The Baseline Rule

It is unlawful to **offer or sell** a security in a state unless the security is **registered**, the security is **exempt**, the transaction is **exempt**, or the security is **federal covered**. Note that the prohibition reaches **offers**, not just completed sales — an offer made before effectiveness is itself a violation.

That the statute reaches offers is not a technicality. The harm the act guards against occurs when an investor forms a decision on the basis of incomplete information, and that happens at the sales pitch, not at settlement. If only completed sales were unlawful, a promoter could pitch freely and paper the trade later.

## The Three State Registration Methods

The three methods answer one question — how much independent state work does this offering need? — and they answer it by asking how much verification has already been done by someone else.

### Registration by Notification (Filing)

The lightest method, available only to **seasoned issuers** with an established operating and earnings history. Requirements typically include several years of continuous operation and a demonstrated record of paying dividends or earnings sufficient to cover fixed charges. The issuer files a short statement and the registration becomes effective **automatically after a short waiting period** (commonly **2 business days** under the model act) if no stop order is pending.

Not every state offers this method; where it exists, it exists because a company with a long clean history poses less disclosure risk. The record itself is the verification — a firm that has operated profitably for years and paid dividends has already demonstrated the things a registration statement is designed to reveal.

### Registration by Coordination

The workhorse for **public offerings registered federally under the Securities Act of 1933**. The issuer files with the state a copy of the federal **prospectus**, plus state-required items (articles of incorporation, underwriting agreements, indenture, opinion of counsel, specimen certificate) and consents to service of process. Amendments to the federal filing must be forwarded promptly.

Coordination exists to prevent a timing absurdity. An offering priced and cleared federally cannot sensibly wait for fifty separate state effectiveness dates; underwriting syndicates form and price on a single date. So the state deliberately **synchronizes** with the federal process rather than running its own clock.

The tested point: a coordinated registration becomes **effective at the same moment the federal registration becomes effective**, provided three conditions hold. Under the model act these are that the registration statement has been **on file with the Administrator for at least 10 days**; that a statement of the **maximum and minimum proposed offering prices** and the **maximum underwriting discounts and commissions** has been on file for **two full business days**; and that **no stop order is in effect** and no proceeding is pending. Adopting states may lengthen the filing period, so a fact pattern giving a different number is not necessarily wrong. **State effectiveness rides on federal effectiveness** — the Administrator does not choose the date.

### Registration by Qualification

The most demanding method and the **default**. Qualification is used for offerings **not registered federally** — intrastate offerings, offerings under exemptions from '33 Act registration — and it may be used by **any** issuer for **any** security. The issuer must file extensive information: the business, capitalization, officers and directors and their compensation and holdings, use of proceeds, financial statements, and copies of organizational and offering documents.

Qualification is demanding because nobody else has looked. There is no federal registration statement and no operating record to lean on, so the state does the entire review itself — which is also why the Administrator, and not a calendar, controls the outcome. **A qualification registration becomes effective when the Administrator so orders.**

- Method: Notification / Filing. Who uses it: Seasoned issuers with an operating history. Purpose: Minimal filing where the track record substitutes for review. When effective: Automatically after a short waiting period.
- Method: Coordination. Who uses it: Issuers concurrently registering with the SEC under the '33 Act. Purpose: Synchronize state clearance with a federal offering. When effective: Simultaneously with the federal registration.
- Method: Qualification. Who uses it: Any issuer; required when there is no federal registration. Purpose: Full state review where no other regulator has examined the offering. When effective: When the Administrator orders it effective.


### Worked Scenario: Which Method?

Three issuers plan offerings in the same state.

**Alder Manufacturing** has been profitable for twelve years, pays a steady dividend, and is doing a small follow-on offering that will not be registered with the SEC. Its operating record fits **notification** where the state offers it; if not, it registers by **qualification**.

**Bracken Robotics** is doing its IPO, registered with the SEC on Form S-1 and underwritten by a syndicate. It registers by **coordination** in each state, and its state effectiveness will occur at the moment the SEC declares the federal registration effective — assuming the state filings have been on file long enough and the price range and underwriting terms were filed on time. If Bracken files in one state only four days before federal effectiveness, that state's registration is **not** effective on the federal date; the offering cannot be sold there until the waiting period runs.

**Corvus Brewing** is a three-year-old company raising $4M entirely from residents of its home state, relying on the federal intrastate exemption. Nothing was filed with the SEC, so there is nothing to coordinate with and no operating record to shortcut the review. Corvus registers by **qualification**, and its offering goes effective only when the Administrator says so.

## The Registration Statement and Administrator Powers

A state registration statement identifies the **amount of securities to be offered in that state**, the states in which the offering is proposed, any adverse order or judgment, the offering price, underwriting discounts, and the expected **use of proceeds**. It must be signed by an appropriate officer and accompanied by a **consent to service of process** appointing the Administrator as attorney for service.

The registration statement may be filed by the **issuer**, by any **selling security holder**, or by a **registered broker-dealer**, and the filing is accompanied by a fee usually calculated on the amount offered in the state.

The Administrator may impose conditions, including:

- **Escrow** of shares issued to promoters, officers, or others within a defined lookback period at a price below the public offering price, until the issuer meets specified conditions;
- **Impoundment** of proceeds — funds held until a specified minimum amount is raised, and returned to purchasers if it is not;
- **Specified use of proceeds**, restricting the issuer to the stated purposes.

The Administrator may also require the offering to be made through an **escrow agent** or a specified form of subscription.

### When Conditions Are Imposed, and Why

Each condition answers a specific structural risk, and knowing which risk tells you which condition a fact pattern calls for.

**Escrow** answers **cheap-stock dilution**. Suppose promoters bought 2 million shares for a tenth of a cent each six months before an offering priced at $5. On day one of trading they hold an enormous paper gain that the public investors funded, and they have every incentive to sell into the new market and walk away — leaving the public holding a company whose founders no longer have a stake. Escrow locks those shares up until the enterprise achieves something, realigning the promoters with the people who paid full price.

**Impoundment** answers the **underfunded venture**. If a company needs $5M to build a plant and raises $900,000, the money is not enough to accomplish the stated purpose but is quite enough to be consumed by salaries and overhead. Impoundment holds subscriptions until the minimum is reached and returns them if it is not — the mechanism behind a minimum-maximum or all-or-none offering.

**Specified use of proceeds** answers **bait and switch** — money raised to build a facility being redirected to repay insider loans.

These conditions appear most often in **qualification** filings by young companies with promoters, no earnings, and a plan requiring a minimum raise, and rarely in a coordinated offering by an established issuer.

### Worked Scenario: A Conditioned Offering

Sable Diagnostics is a four-year-old company with no revenue, registering by qualification to raise $6M to complete clinical work on a device. Its two founders acquired 3 million shares at $0.005 nine months ago; the public price is $4. The prospectus says the program requires at least $4M to reach the next milestone.

The Administrator does not deny the registration — the offering is speculative, but speculation fully disclosed is not a ground for denial. Instead it conditions effectiveness: the founders' shares go into **escrow** until the company reaches specified milestones, subscriptions are **impounded** until $4M is raised and returned to investors if it is not, and the issuer is restricted to the **stated use of proceeds**. The risk is not eliminated; it is disclosed and structured so that the investors and the promoters face it together.

### Merit Review and Its Limits

State registration is historically **merit review** — the Administrator can assess fairness, not just disclosure. But the model act constrains this: the Administrator **may not deny registration solely because the offering is speculative or risky** when the risks are **fully and adequately disclosed**. The remedy for a speculative offering is disclosure and possibly escrow or impoundment, not denial. A denial must rest on a finding such as inadequate disclosure, unreasonable underwriting compensation, unfair promoter benefits, or a filing that is incomplete or misleading.

The federal side takes the principle further. **The SEC does not approve securities and does not pass on their merits.** An effective registration means only that the disclosure appeared adequate on its face, and it is expressly unlawful to represent otherwise — telling a customer that a security is "SEC approved" is a violation, not a puff. The whole federal design rests on the judgment that regulators are poor at picking investments and good at forcing the facts into the open, so the investor decides. State merit review is the older, narrower exception to that philosophy, and even it stops short of protecting investors from risk they were told about.

### Stop Orders

The Administrator may issue a **stop order** denying, suspending, or revoking the effectiveness of a registration statement. The grounds are the mirror image of the grant: the filing is incomplete, false, or misleading in a material respect; the offering would work a fraud; the issuer's method of business is unlawful; the offering is being made on terms that are unfair, unjust, or inequitable; underwriting compensation or promoter benefits are unreasonable; or an applicable order or injunction is outstanding.

Two procedural features are tested. First, a stop order generally requires **prior notice, an opportunity for a hearing, and written findings of fact and conclusions of law** — the Administrator can act summarily in some circumstances, but the affected party is entitled to a hearing promptly afterward. Second, the Administrator may **vacate or modify a stop order** if the conditions that prompted it no longer exist, so a curable defect can be cured.

Note the sequencing trap: a stop order entered against a filing that has not yet gone effective is a **denial**; one entered afterward is a **suspension or revocation**. And a stop order may not be entered on the basis of a fact the Administrator knew when the registration was allowed to become effective.

### Effective Period and Amendments

A state registration is generally effective for **one year** from the effective date. It may continue while securities remain unsold if the offering is still being made by the underwriter. The registrant files **reports** on the progress of the offering as the Administrator requires (not more often than quarterly under the model act), and the amount registered may be **increased** by amendment on payment of the additional fee.

A registration statement may not be **withdrawn** for a period after effectiveness while securities remain unsold, and the offering price and underwriting terms in an effective registration may generally be changed only by a post-effective amendment.

## Federal Covered Securities

**NSMIA (1996)** preempted state registration of a defined class of **federal covered securities**:

- Securities **listed on a national exchange** — NYSE, Nasdaq, NYSE American, and others designated by SEC rule — **and any security equal or senior in rank** to a listed security of the same issuer. So if common stock is listed, that issuer's preferred stock, bonds, warrants, and rights are also covered.
- Securities issued by an **investment company registered under the Investment Company Act of 1940** — open-end funds, closed-end funds, UITs.
- Securities sold to **qualified purchasers** as defined by the SEC.
- **Rule 506** offerings under **Regulation D** — both **506(b)** and **506(c)**.
- **Regulation A+ Tier 2** offerings (up to $75M in 12 months) sold to qualified purchasers.
- Securities issued in certain **exempt transactions**, and securities issued by the **US government**.

The organizing idea behind the list is that each category has already been vetted by a gatekeeper the states could not improve on: an exchange's listing standards, the Investment Company Act's structural requirements, the sophistication of a qualified purchaser, or a federal offering framework with its own filing regime. Where such a gatekeeper exists, a second review by fifty administrators adds cost without adding protection.

### What the States Kept

Preemption removed registration authority, not everything:

- States may require **notice filings** — typically a copy of the federal filing, a consent to service of process, and a **fee**.
- States retain **full antifraud authority** over federal covered securities.
- States may investigate and bring enforcement actions for fraud or deceit.
- States continue to register the **broker-dealers and agents** who sell covered securities, and may deny, suspend, or revoke those registrations.

A notice filing is not a registration and gives the state no merit-review power: the Administrator may not review the offering's fairness and may not condition or deny it. What the notice filing does is preserve the two things preemption did not touch — the state knows who is selling within its borders, and it has a consent to service of process on file when it needs to sue.

**Rule 504 is NOT federal covered.** This is the classic trap. Rule 504 permits up to **$10M** in 12 months but was never given preemption, so **504 offerings must be registered or exempt at the state level** — which is precisely why 504 is often paired with a state-level exemption or with the model act's limited offering exemption. Rule 506 is covered; Rule 504 is not. Likewise **Reg A+ Tier 1** is not preempted; only **Tier 2** is.

The reasoning behind the 504/506 split is worth carrying: Rule 506 has no dollar ceiling but restricts sales to accredited investors (with a limited allowance for sophisticated purchasers under 506(b)), so the federal rule itself screens the buyers. Rule 504 caps the dollars but does not screen the buyers the same way — so the states kept it. **Preemption follows the quality of the federal screen, not the size of the offering.**

## The Federal Acts at a High Level

The **Securities Act of 1933** — the "paper act" — governs the **issuance** of securities in the primary market. Its central mechanism is the **registration statement** filed with the SEC and the **prospectus** delivered to purchasers, containing the material facts an investor needs. The SEC does **not** approve securities or pass on their merits; an effective registration means the disclosure appeared adequate, and it is unlawful to represent otherwise.

The **Securities Exchange Act of 1934** — the "people act" — governs the **secondary market** and the participants in it: it created the **SEC**, requires registration of exchanges, broker-dealers, and associated persons, mandates ongoing issuer reporting (10-K, 10-Q, 8-K), regulates proxies, insider reporting and short-swing profits, credit and margin through Regulation T, and contains the general antifraud provision **§10(b)** and **Rule 10b-5**.

## The questions this topic is tested with

**Registration by qualification differs from the other two methods of state securities registration in that it:**

Qualification is the most burdensome method, is available to any issuer, and is the method used when no federal registration statement is involved — an intrastate offering, for example. It becomes effective only when the Administrator orders it effective.

**Larkspur Foods has filed a registration statement with the SEC for its initial public offering and plans to sell shares in eleven states. The shares will trade over the counter and will not be listed on any national securities exchange. The appropriate method of state registration is:**

Coordination is used when the same offering is registered with the SEC under the Securities Act of 1933 and simultaneously registered in the states; state effectiveness occurs automatically at the moment federal effectiveness occurs, provided the filing conditions are met. Registration with the SEC alone does not make a security federal covered — exchange listing or a specific NSMIA category does.

**With respect to a federal covered security, the National Securities Markets Improvement Act permits a state Administrator to:**

NSMIA preempts state registration and merit review of federal covered securities, but the states keep the power to collect notice filings and fees and to police fraud. Preemption of registration is never preemption of the antifraud provisions.

**Fenwick Industries raises $4 million in a Regulation D offering conducted under Rule 504. For state law purposes the securities are:**

Only Rule 506 offerings are federal covered securities under NSMIA. Rule 504 offerings are not preempted, so the issuer must satisfy state registration or locate a state exemption in every state in which it sells.

**A state securities registration statement that has become effective generally remains effective for:**

A registration statement is effective for one year from its effective date and may run past the year while the underwriters continue distributing the same offering. Do not confuse this with registration of persons, which expires on December 31 and must be renewed annually.

**A pre-revenue biotechnology company files a registration by qualification. The prospectus discloses prominently that investors could lose their entire investment. The Administrator considers the offering highly speculative. The Administrator:**

Disclosure rather than merit controls the outcome: an Administrator may not deny an otherwise complete registration merely because the investment is risky when the risks are fully and prominently disclosed. No Administrator ever approves or endorses the merits of a security, and representing otherwise is itself a prohibited practice.
