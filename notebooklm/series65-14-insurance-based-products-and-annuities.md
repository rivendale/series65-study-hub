# Insurance-Based Products and Annuities

This is one topic from the Series 65 exam, the Uniform Investment Adviser Law
Examination. It is worth about 3 percent of the exam.

## Why this topic matters

Insurance products transfer risk; annuities transfer longevity risk. The exam tests what distinguishes term from permanent coverage, how a separate account makes a product a security, the difference between accumulation units and annuity units, the trade-off among payout options, and the LIFO ordinary-income tax treatment of non-qualified annuity withdrawals.

## Why an adviser needs this

Insurance is a **risk-transfer contract**, not primarily an investment. A policyholder pays a small certain cost to avoid a large uncertain one, and the insurer accepts that uncertainty because pooling thousands of independent risks makes the aggregate predictable even though no single case is. That is the whole idea, and it explains why insurance is priced from mortality tables rather than from expected returns.

When a product's value depends on the performance of a separate investment account, however, it becomes a **security** as well as an insurance contract — and it lands squarely inside an adviser's suitability and disclosure obligations. Most exam points come from that intersection.

Annuities invert the same machinery. Life insurance protects against **dying too soon**; an annuity protects against **living too long**. Both are longevity bets with the insurer on the other side, which is why one company can write both and why the mortality pool that makes life insurance work is the same pool that lets an annuity pay more than a bond of comparable safety: the annuitants who die early subsidize those who live long. That subsidy — the **mortality credit** — is what an annuity actually sells, and it is unavailable in any securities product.

## Life insurance types

### Term

Pure death benefit for a stated period. **No cash value.** Premiums are lowest per dollar of coverage at younger ages and rise sharply at renewal as the insured ages. **Level term** keeps the premium and face amount fixed for the term; **decreasing term** reduces the death benefit over time and is often used to cover a mortgage. **Convertibility** lets the owner exchange the policy for permanent coverage without new evidence of insurability. Term is the right answer when the need is **temporary and the budget is tight** — income replacement for young children, for example.

### Whole life (permanent, ordinary)

Coverage for life with a **guaranteed level premium**, a **guaranteed death benefit**, and a **guaranteed minimum cash value** that builds on a fixed schedule. The insurer bears the investment risk and invests in its **general account**. Cash value grows **tax-deferred**; the owner may take **policy loans** against it, which reduce the death benefit if unpaid but are not taxable while the policy stays in force. Whole life is inflexible and expensive per dollar of coverage — that inflexibility is exactly what the exam rewards you for identifying.

Why does a level premium work at all when the risk of death climbs every year? Because the early premiums deliberately **overcharge** relative to that year's mortality cost, and the excess accumulates as reserve. Cash value is not a side benefit bolted on; it is the mathematical residue of prepaying for coverage. That also explains why surrendering a whole life policy in its first years returns so little — acquisition costs and commissions are recovered from those same early premiums.

### Universal life

Permanent coverage with **flexible premiums and an adjustable death benefit**. Payments go into a cash value account credited with interest at a rate the insurer declares (subject to a guaranteed floor); mortality and expense charges are deducted monthly. The flexibility is real but so is the danger: **if the cash value is exhausted by charges, the policy lapses**. Universal life is not a security — the general account bears the investment risk.

### Variable life

Permanent coverage whose cash value — and death benefit above a guaranteed minimum — depends on **subaccounts in the insurer's separate account** chosen by the policyowner. The **policyowner bears the investment risk**, so a variable policy is **a security and an insurance product**, requiring a **prospectus** and, for the person selling it, both **securities registration and a state insurance license**. Premiums are typically fixed and scheduled.

### Variable universal life (VUL)

The combination: **flexible premiums** of universal life plus **separate-account investment** of variable life. Maximum flexibility, maximum owner-borne risk, and the highest layered cost. Also a security.

### Comparing them on the things that matter

- Product: Term. Cost per dollar of coverage: Lowest initially, rises steeply at renewal. Premium flexibility: Fixed for the term. Cash value: None. Who bears investment risk: Neither — no investment element. Security?: No.
- Product: Whole life. Cost per dollar of coverage: High and level for life. Premium flexibility: None — pay as scheduled. Cash value: Guaranteed, on a set schedule. Who bears investment risk: Insurer. Security?: No.
- Product: Universal life. Cost per dollar of coverage: Moderate. Premium flexibility: Flexible premium and adjustable death benefit. Cash value: Credited at a declared rate with a guaranteed floor. Who bears investment risk: Insurer (subject to the floor). Security?: No.
- Product: Variable life. Cost per dollar of coverage: High, with layered charges. Premium flexibility: Scheduled premium. Cash value: Rides subaccounts; no floor. Who bears investment risk: Policyowner. Security?: Yes.
- Product: VUL. Cost per dollar of coverage: Highest, most layers of charges. Premium flexibility: Flexible. Cash value: Rides subaccounts; no floor. Who bears investment risk: Policyowner. Security?: Yes.


Read the table as a single trade: **every dollar of guarantee is bought with a dollar of cost or flexibility**. Term guarantees nothing beyond the term and is cheap. Whole life guarantees everything and is rigid and expensive. Universal life buys flexibility by removing the guarantee that the policy stays funded. Variable products buy upside by handing the investment risk to the client.

### The separate account concept

A **separate account** is an insulated pool of assets held apart from the insurer's general account and invested according to subaccount objectives. It is the dividing line the exam cares about: **general account = insurer takes the investment risk, not a security; separate account = client takes the investment risk, is a security.**

The insulation matters for a practical reason: separate account assets are held for the benefit of contract owners and are generally **not reachable by the insurer's general creditors**. A fixed annuity owner, by contrast, is a general creditor of the insurer, which is why the **claims-paying ability of the carrier** is the relevant risk on a fixed product and largely irrelevant on the investment performance of a variable one.

## Policy loans and the taxation of life insurance

**Policy loans** are borrowed against the cash value, and the insurer charges interest. They are **not taxable while the policy remains in force**, because a loan is not income — the owner has an offsetting obligation. Unpaid loans plus accrued interest **reduce the death benefit** dollar for dollar.

The trap sits at the end. If the policy **lapses or is surrendered with a loan outstanding**, the loan is treated as received, and the owner recognizes gain to the extent total distributions (including the loan) exceed the premiums paid. A client who borrowed and spent the money years earlier can receive a tax bill with no cash attached. Advisers should watch aging universal life policies where loan interest is quietly eating the account value.

The **death benefit itself is generally received income-tax-free** by the beneficiary. Three qualifications the exam likes:

- **Income-tax-free is not estate-tax-free.** If the insured held any **incident of ownership** at death — the right to change beneficiaries, borrow, or surrender — the proceeds are includable in the gross estate. Ownership by an irrevocable trust or another person is the standard fix.
- **Interest is taxable.** If proceeds are left with the insurer or paid under a settlement option, the interest component is ordinary income even though the principal is not.
- **Transfer for value.** If a policy is sold for consideration, the death benefit generally becomes taxable to the buyer above the consideration and premiums paid, subject to statutory exceptions.

Finally, **modified endowment contracts (MECs)**. A permanent policy funded faster than the seven-pay limit permits keeps its income-tax-free death benefit but loses its favorable living-benefit treatment: distributions and even **loans** are taxed **LIFO — earnings first** — with a 10% penalty before age 59½, exactly like an annuity. Overfunding a policy to make it "a better investment" is the direct route to MEC status.

## Annuities: the structure

An annuity is a contract with an insurance company designed to **provide income the owner cannot outlive**. Two phases:

- **Accumulation phase** — money goes in (single premium or periodic), grows tax-deferred, and the owner may withdraw or surrender. In a variable contract, purchase payments buy **accumulation units**, whose number rises with each contribution and whose value floats with subaccount performance.
- **Annuitization (payout) phase** — accumulation units are converted to a **fixed number of annuity units** based on the account value, the payout option, the assumed interest rate, and mortality tables. From then on **the number of annuity units never changes**; the **value per unit fluctuates**, so the check varies. Annuitization is generally **irrevocable** and ends access to the lump sum.

The reversal of what varies is the whole point, and it is worth saying out loud. **Going in, the count moves and reflects contributions. Coming out, the count is frozen and only price moves.** Nothing else could work: the insurer has promised to pay for life, so it must fix the number of units it owes each period and let market value do the rest.

### The assumed interest rate

The size of the first variable payment is set using an **assumed interest rate (AIR)**, a benchmark the insurer builds into the payout calculation. It is **not a guarantee and not a floor**. Every period thereafter, the separate account's actual net performance is measured **against the AIR**:

- Actual performance **above** the AIR — the annuity unit value rises and the **next payment is larger**.
- Actual performance **exactly equal** to the AIR — the unit value is unchanged and the **payment stays the same** (it does not "catch up").
- Actual performance **below** the AIR — the unit value falls and the **payment shrinks**, even if the account earned a positive return.

That third line is the exam's favorite, and the reason a higher AIR is not a better deal: a high AIR produces a larger first check and a payment stream that is harder to sustain.

### A variable payout under a 4% AIR

A contract annuitizes into **100 annuity units** with an annuity unit value of $10.00, so the first annual payment is 100 × $10.00 = **$1,000**. The AIR is **4%**.

In year one the separate account returns **9.2%** net. Compare it to the AIR: the unit value moves by the ratio 1.092 / 1.04 = **1.05**, so the annuity unit value rises to $10.00 × 1.05 = **$10.50** and the payment becomes 100 × $10.50 = **$1,050**.

In year two the account returns **exactly 4.0%**. The ratio is 1.04 / 1.04 = **1.00**, the unit value stays at **$10.50**, and the payment stays at **$1,050**. Matching the AIR holds the check level; it does not raise it.

In year three the account returns **+1.92%** — a gain, but below the AIR. The ratio is 1.0192 / 1.04 = **0.98**, the unit value falls to $10.50 × 0.98 = **$10.29**, and the payment drops to 100 × $10.29 = **$1,029**.

Throughout, the number of annuity units stayed at 100. **A positive return cut the client's income because it was smaller than the AIR** — that is the point, and a client who is told a variable annuity "can only go up if the market goes up" has been misled.

### Fixed, variable, and indexed

- **Fixed annuity** — the insurer guarantees a minimum interest rate and a fixed dollar payout from its **general account**. The insurer bears investment risk; the owner bears **purchasing power (inflation) risk**. Not a security.
- **Variable annuity** — payments are invested in **separate account subaccounts**; the owner bears investment risk and payouts vary. **A security**, sold with a prospectus, requiring securities and insurance licensing.
- **Indexed (equity-indexed) annuity** — credits interest tied to a market index subject to a **participation rate, a cap, and a guaranteed minimum floor**. Sold as insurance in most states rather than as a federally registered security; regulators watch these closely because the crediting formulas are opaque and surrender periods are long.

### How an indexed annuity actually credits interest

The insurer is not investing the client's money in the index. It buys bonds with almost all of the premium and spends the remainder on **index call options**; the option budget is what the crediting formula can afford, and every limiting term exists to keep the formula inside that budget:

- **Participation rate** — the fraction of the index move counted, for example 70% of a 20% rise.
- **Cap** — a hard ceiling on the credit for the period.
- **Spread, margin, or asset fee** — a percentage subtracted from the index move before crediting.
- **Floor** — the guaranteed minimum, commonly 0%, which is what makes the product feel safe.

Two further mechanics decide outcomes. **Crediting method**: annual point-to-point compares beginning and ending index values once a year, while monthly-sum and other methods can produce a zero credit in a year the index rose. **Index credits are normally based on price movement only and exclude dividends**, so the comparison against "the market" is not like for like.

Participation rates, caps, and spreads are usually **declared by the insurer periodically and can be lowered** for future periods within contractual limits — a client who bought on an attractive first-year cap has no assurance it survives. Note one boundary: **registered index-linked annuities (RILAs, or buffered annuities)** trade the 0% floor for a buffer against a stated amount of loss and expose the owner to losses beyond it. Because the owner can lose principal, these **are registered with the SEC and sold with a prospectus** — the label "indexed" alone does not settle whether a product is a security.

### An indexed annuity through three index years

A client places **$100,000** in a contract with a **70% participation rate**, an **8% annual cap**, and a **0% floor**, credited on annual point-to-point.

**Year one:** the index rises **20%**. Apply the participation rate: 20% × 0.70 = **14%**. Apply the cap: 14% exceeds 8%, so the credit is **8%**. The account becomes $100,000 × 1.08 = **$108,000**.

**Year two:** the index falls **15%**. The floor applies, so the credit is **0%** and the account stays at **$108,000**. Meanwhile a direct index investment would have gone $100,000 × 1.20 = $120,000, then × 0.85 = **$102,000**. After two years the annuity is **$6,000 ahead**, and this is the scenario the sales presentation shows.

**Year three:** the index rises **30%**. Participation gives 30% × 0.70 = 21%, the cap cuts it to **8%**, and the account reaches $108,000 × 1.08 = **$116,640**. The direct investment goes $102,000 × 1.30 = **$132,600** — now **$15,960 ahead**.

The floor paid for itself in the bad year and the cap charged for it twice over in the good ones. That is the honest description of an indexed annuity: **it is not equity exposure with a safety net, it is a bond-like return with a shot at more**, and the comparison also ignores dividends the index investor would have received.

### Immediate vs deferred

An **immediate annuity** is purchased with a single premium and begins payments within about a year — no accumulation phase. A **deferred annuity** accumulates first and pays later; only deferred contracts have a surrender-charge schedule and an accumulation phase.

## Payout options

The rule that decides most questions: **the more the insurer guarantees, the smaller each payment.**

- Option: Life only (straight life). What it promises: Income for the annuitant's life; nothing to anyone at death. Relative payment: Largest payment.
- Option: Life with period certain. What it promises: Life, but at least a set number of years to a beneficiary. Relative payment: Smaller.
- Option: Unit (or cash) refund. What it promises: Life, plus the remainder of the principal to a beneficiary. Relative payment: Smaller.
- Option: Joint and last survivor. What it promises: Income until the second of two people dies. Relative payment: Smallest payment.


Life only pays most because the insurer's obligation ends at death; joint and last survivor pays least because two lives must be covered. A **period certain only** option (payments for a set term, no life contingency) is not a true life annuity at all.

The mechanism behind the ranking is the mortality pool. Under **life only**, the account of an annuitant who dies in year two stays in the pool and funds the payments of those who live to ninety-five — those forfeited balances are the **mortality credit** that lets the payment exceed what the same sum could safely withdraw on its own. Every guarantee added to the contract claws some of that credit back for the annuitant's estate, and the check falls accordingly. Ask "how much money can the insurer keep?" and the ranking writes itself.

Two distinctions worth separating:

- **Joint life** stops at the **first** death; **joint and last survivor** continues until the **second**. They sound alike and pay very differently, because covering two lives to the second death is the longest obligation on the list.
- **Life with period certain** guarantees a number of *years*; **cash or unit refund** guarantees a number of *dollars* — the remaining principal. One promises time, the other promises money.

**Where the analysis flips:** the more guarantees a client demands, the closer the annuity moves to being an expensive bond ladder. A client who wants a large death benefit and a guaranteed income stream is describing two products, and buying life insurance plus a life-only annuity is often cheaper than buying one annuity that tries to do both.

## Costs and features

- **Surrender charges** — a declining schedule (often 7 to 10 years, sometimes longer) imposed on withdrawals above a free amount during the early years of a deferred contract. Long surrender periods on an older client are a red flag. The charge is not arbitrary: the insurer paid the selling commission on day one and recovers it out of contract charges over time, so the schedule tracks the **unamortized commission**. That is why a longer surrender period usually signals a **larger commission**, and why the schedule is the single most informative page in the contract.
- **Mortality and expense (M&E) risk charge** — an ongoing asset-based fee compensating the insurer for guaranteeing lifetime payments regardless of how long the annuitant lives and for holding expense charges level.
- Also expect **administrative fees, subaccount management fees, and rider charges** for guaranteed income or death benefits. Layered costs are why annuity expenses commonly exceed comparable mutual fund costs.
- **Section 1035 exchange** — a **tax-free exchange** of one annuity or life policy for another of like kind. It preserves cost basis and defers gain, but it **does not reset surrender charges or fees** and often starts a **new surrender period**. Repeated exchanges to generate commissions are **churning/twisting** and a classic violation. Direction matters and is tested: **life to life, life to annuity, and annuity to annuity all qualify — annuity to life does not.** The logic is one-way: you may move into a contract with more embedded income, never out of one into a policy whose death benefit escapes income tax.

### The bonus that costs more than it pays

A client is shown a deferred annuity offering a **5% premium bonus**. On a $100,000 purchase the bonus is 5% × $100,000 = **$5,000**, so the contract starts at **$105,000** rather than $100,000. The bonus contract carries a mortality and expense charge of **1.65%** and a **12-year** surrender schedule; a comparable contract from the same insurer without the bonus charges **1.05%** and surrenders over **7 years**.

Price the difference. The extra ongoing charge is 1.65% − 1.05% = **0.60%** a year, which on the $105,000 starting value is 0.006 × $105,000 = **$630** in the first year. Divide the bonus by the annual excess: $5,000 / $630 = **7.9 years**. Before the end of year eight the extra charges have consumed the entire bonus — and the client still has four more years before she can leave without a surrender charge.

Two features make it worse rather than merely even. Bonus contracts commonly include a **recapture provision** that takes the bonus back on early surrender, and the higher charge is assessed on a **growing** account value, so the annual cost rises above $630 as the contract appreciates. The bonus is not a gift; it is a prepayment the client finances at an interest rate she was never quoted.

## Taxation of annuities

- Growth inside the contract is **tax-deferred**; there is **no annual 1099 for internal gains**.
- On a **non-qualified** annuity (purchased with after-tax dollars), the cost basis is not taxed again, but partial withdrawals come out **earnings first — LIFO** — so early withdrawals are **fully taxable** until earnings are exhausted.
- All taxable amounts are **ordinary income, never long-term capital gain**, no matter how long the contract was held or what the subaccounts held. This is the single most-missed annuity tax point.
- Withdrawals before **age 59½** generally incur a **10% penalty** on the taxable portion, on top of ordinary income tax, subject to statutory exceptions.
- Annuitized payments are split by an **exclusion ratio**: the portion representing return of basis is tax-free, the rest is ordinary income, until basis is fully recovered.
- **No step-up in basis at death** — an inherited annuity carries embedded ordinary income to the beneficiary, unlike appreciated stock.

Why does annuitizing change the ordering? Because a lump-sum withdrawal is discretionary and the tax rules refuse to let a client cherry-pick basis, while an annuitized stream is a fixed contract under which basis is recovered **ratably over the expected payments**. Once the client gives up control, the law gives back proportional basis recovery. The ratio is set once at annuitization and does not change: **outlive the life expectancy and every later payment is 100% taxable**, because the basis is gone; die before recovering it and the unrecovered amount is deductible on the final return.

**The boundary that catches people:** all of this describes a **non-qualified** annuity, bought with after-tax dollars. A **qualified** annuity inside an IRA or employer plan holds pre-tax dollars, so there is **no investment in the contract and no exclusion ratio** — every dollar of every payment is ordinary income, and required minimum distributions apply at age 73. Reaching for the exclusion ratio on a qualified contract is a wasted calculation.

## Suitability concerns

State regulators bring more annuity cases than almost any other product category. Watch for:

- **A variable or deferred annuity inside an IRA or 401(k)** — tax deferral is redundant, and the client pays annuity costs for a benefit already provided.
- **Long surrender periods relative to age, health, or liquidity needs**, especially for a client who may need principal within the surrender window.
- **Replacement or 1035 exchange** that restarts surrender charges without a demonstrable benefit.
- **Costs and riders not clearly disclosed**, or performance illustrated as if guaranteed.
- A variable annuity's returns fluctuate; only the mortality guarantee is a guarantee. **Calling a variable annuity "safe" or "guaranteed" is a misrepresentation.**

### When an annuity is the wrong recommendation

Beyond the obvious cases, three arguments defeat an annuity recommendation even when the client can afford it:

- **The tax treatment can be a downgrade.** Equity exposure held in a taxable account produces long-term capital gains and qualified dividends and receives a **step-up in basis at death**. The same exposure inside a variable annuity converts all of it into **ordinary income** and forfeits the step-up. For a long-horizon equity investor, deferral can cost more than it saves.
- **The horizon is too short for the surrender schedule.** A client who may need principal inside the surrender period is buying an illiquid contract with a penalty attached, and if she is under 59½ the 10% federal penalty stacks on top of the contract charge.
- **A cheaper structure does the same job.** If the goal is guaranteed lifetime income, a **single premium immediate annuity** delivers it with far less complexity and cost than a deferred variable contract wrapped in living-benefit riders.

The mirror case matters too. An annuity is genuinely appropriate for a client who has **exhausted qualified plan capacity**, has a **long deferral horizon**, and above all fears **outliving her assets** — the mortality credit is a real benefit no mutual fund can replicate, and the beneficiary designation passes the contract outside probate. The correct answer is rarely "annuities are bad"; it is "this annuity, for this client, at this cost."

## The questions this topic is tested with

**Which type of life insurance provides a death benefit and a cash value whose performance depends on subaccounts held in the insurer's separate account, with the policyowner bearing the investment risk?**

Variable life places cash value in separate account subaccounts, so the policyowner bears investment risk and the contract is a security requiring both securities and insurance licensing. Term builds no cash value, whole life guarantees a fixed cash value in the general account, and universal life offers flexible premiums with an interest-crediting general account.

**In a variable annuity, what happens at the moment the contract is annuitized?**

During accumulation the contract buys a varying number of accumulation units. At annuitization those units are exchanged for a fixed number of annuity units, and each payment then varies because the value per annuity unit changes with subaccount performance measured against the assumed interest rate.

**A 68-year-old retiree with no dependents and no beneficiaries to protect wants the largest possible monthly income from his annuity contract. Which settlement option should be recommended, and what is the trade-off?**

Life only produces the highest monthly payment of any option because the insurer has no obligation after the annuitant dies. Every guarantee added — period certain, refund, or a second life — reduces the payment. With no beneficiaries to protect, the trade-off is acceptable here.

**A client annuitizes a non-qualified annuity. His investment in the contract is $120,000 and his expected total return over the payout period is $300,000. He receives $1,500 per month. How much of each monthly payment is taxable?**

The exclusion ratio is investment in the contract ÷ expected return = $120,000 ÷ $300,000 = 40%. So 40% of each payment ($600) is a tax-free return of basis and the remaining 60% ($900) is taxable as ordinary income. Once total basis is recovered, payments become fully taxable.

**A 52-year-old client makes a $30,000 partial withdrawal from a non-qualified variable annuity purchased with $100,000 that is now worth $160,000. How is the withdrawal treated for federal tax purposes?**

Non-qualified annuity withdrawals come out on a LIFO basis, so earnings are distributed first. The contract holds $60,000 of gain, so the full $30,000 is earnings, taxed as ordinary income — never capital gain — and a 10% premature distribution penalty applies below age 59½.

**A client owns a variable annuity purchased four years ago that is still within a seven-year surrender charge schedule. An agent proposes exchanging it for a new variable annuity with a similar subaccount lineup and a fresh seven-year surrender schedule, noting the transaction qualifies as a 1035 exchange. What is the principal concern?**

Section 1035 permits annuity-to-annuity exchanges without current taxation, so the tax treatment is not the issue. The problem is suitability: paying a surrender charge and starting a new surrender period for essentially the same product suggests the exchange serves the agent rather than the client.
