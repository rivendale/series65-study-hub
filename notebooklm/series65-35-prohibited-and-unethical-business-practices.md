# Prohibited and Unethical Business Practices

This is one topic from the Series 65 exam, the Uniform Investment Adviser Law
Examination. It is worth about 4 percent of the exam.

## Why this topic matters

NASAA's statements of policy on dishonest and unethical practices give the Administrator authority to discipline an adviser or representative even where no fraud can be proven, and this material generates more exam questions than any other single ethics topic.

## Why this list matters

Fraud requires proof of a material misstatement or a scheme. **Dishonest or unethical practices** require far less. NASAA's statements of policy list conduct that is grounds for **denial, suspension, or revocation** of a registration on its own. An Administrator can act on an unethical practice without ever alleging fraud, and the standard is judged against **industry custom and the duty owed to the client**, not against the adviser's good intentions.

Investment advisers and investment adviser representatives are **fiduciaries**. That baseline colors every item below: the question is almost always "did the adviser put the client first, and did the client know everything a reasonable client would want to know?"

## Unethical, fraudulent, or both

The two categories overlap without coinciding, and questions are written on the gap.

**Fraud** under the anti-fraud provisions requires a **material misstatement or omission, or a scheme to defraud, in connection with the offer, sale, or purchase of a security** — and in a private action, **scienter**. It carries criminal exposure, civil liability to the client, and rescission.

**A dishonest or unethical practice** requires none of that. It is defined by conduct falling below the standard the client and the industry are entitled to expect, and it supports **administrative** action — denial, suspension, revocation, censure, bar — on its own.

Three relationships to hold:

- **Unethical without being fraudulent.** Exercising discretion without written authority where every trade was sound and profitable. Failing to update a client profile. Charging a fee that is unreasonable but fully disclosed. Advertising as "fee-only" while taking a disclosed insurance override. No misstatement, no scheme, often no loss — and still grounds to revoke a registration.
- **Fraudulent and therefore also unethical.** Converting client funds, scalping, deliberately understating a security's risk. Fraud essentially always breaches the ethics rules too.
- **Neither.** Merely poor service — a slow return call, an unimpressive quarter — is not a violation of anything.

Two consequences follow. The Administrator need not prove intent, reliance, or client loss to act on an unethical practice, which is why these rules are the workhorse of state enforcement. And **the client's satisfaction does not decide the question**: a delighted client does not cure unauthorized trading, and a profitable unauthorized trade is still unauthorized.

## Suitability and recommendation failures

- **Unsuitable recommendations** — advice inconsistent with the client's stated objectives, financial situation, risk tolerance, time horizon, tax status, or liquidity needs. Recommending an illiquid private placement to a retiree who needs income is the archetype.
- **Failing to update the client profile** or making recommendations after the client's circumstances have plainly changed.
- **Churning / excessive trading** — trading whose **frequency, size, or cost is excessive in light of the client's objectives and account**, where the adviser controls the account. The three elements the exam looks for are **control, excessive activity, and intent to generate compensation**. Fee-based accounts can be churned in reverse: **reverse churning** is charging an ongoing wrap or advisory fee on an account that requires little or no activity when a commission account would cost the client far less.
- **Recommending securities without a reasonable basis** — no research, no understanding of the product, reliance on rumor.

**Where the churning line actually falls.** There is no magic turnover number. The analysis is comparative: a **turnover ratio** and a **cost-to-equity ratio** measured against what the client's stated objective would require. An aggressive growth account traded twelve times a year may be entirely defensible; a capital-preservation account traded twelve times a year is not, on identical activity. Two boundaries. The nearest case that comes out the other way is the **client who directs the trading** — where the client rather than the adviser controls the account, the control element fails and the charge becomes unsuitable recommendation, not churning. And churning does not require commissions: **excessive trading in a fee-based account** can still breach the duty through tax drag, spreads, and market impact.

## Trading authority violations

- **Unauthorized trading** — effecting any transaction the client did not authorize.
- **Exercising discretion without the required written authority.** An **investment adviser** may generally act on **oral authority for up to 10 business days** after the first discretionary transaction while the written authorization is obtained. A **broker-dealer agent gets no such grace period** and needs the written authority **before** the first discretionary trade. Reverse those two and you will miss the question.
- **The time-and-price exception.** If the client specifies the **security, the quantity, and whether to buy or sell**, and leaves only **when and at what price** to the adviser, that is **not** discretion and needs **no written authorization**. It is good only for the day it is given unless the client agrees otherwise. Memorize the four decisions: **Action, Asset, Amount** require written discretion; **Time and Price** do not.
- **Exceeding the scope of the authority granted** — trading options in an account where discretion was limited to equities, or trading in an account where authority had been revoked.

## Money, assets, and self-dealing

- **Borrowing money or securities from a client**, unless the client is a **broker-dealer, an affiliate of the adviser, or a financial institution in the business of lending** (for example, the client is a bank and the loan is a normal commercial loan).
- **Lending money to a client**, unless the adviser is a broker-dealer extending margin in the ordinary course or a financial institution in the business of lending.
- **Commingling** client funds or securities with the adviser's own assets — client money must be segregated at a qualified custodian, never in the firm operating account.
- **Conversion or misappropriation** of client funds or securities. This is theft and is also criminal fraud.
- **Guaranteeing performance or guaranteeing a client against loss.** No adviser may promise a specific return or agree to reimburse losses. Note the contrast: a **security whose payments are guaranteed by a third party** (a parent company, an insurer, the U.S. government) may be described as guaranteed **as to what the third party actually guarantees**, with the identity of the guarantor and the limits of the guarantee disclosed. A guarantee of *the client's investment result* is prohibited; describing a *contractual guarantee embedded in the security* is not.
- **Sharing in profits and losses** in a client account — prohibited for agents absent joint account, written approval, and proportionate contribution; for advisers, the analogous problem arises with improper **performance fees** charged to clients who are not qualified.

**Where the borrowing line falls.** The prohibition targets the **dependency** the loan creates: a client who has lent money to her adviser cannot fire him freely, and an adviser who owes a client money cannot advise that client disinterestedly. That is why every exception is a lender **in the business of lending**, where the loan is arm's length and the dependency is purely commercial. Borderline cases worth knowing: a loan from a client who is also a **close family member** is treated leniently under FINRA-style agent rules but strictly under the NASAA adviser rules, so read for which regime governs. A **gift** is not a loan, but a gift large enough to influence advice is a conflict. And being named a **beneficiary** in a client's will, or as executor or trustee, is a conflict most firms prohibit outright — and, if accepted, is also custody.

## Conflicts, disclosure, and compensation

- **Failing to disclose material conflicts of interest** — the fiduciary duty requires **full and fair disclosure of all material facts**, including any conflict that could reasonably affect the advice. Silence is the violation; the conflict itself is often permissible once disclosed and, where required, consented to.
- **Undisclosed compensation** — revenue sharing, 12b-1 fees, soft dollars outside the safe harbor, referral payments, or commissions from an affiliated broker-dealer that the client is not told about.
- **Deceptive fee practices** — charging fees not disclosed in the advisory contract or brochure, fees unreasonable in relation to the services actually provided, or fees materially higher than those charged comparable clients without explanation. There is **no fixed cap** on advisory fees; the test is **reasonableness relative to services and to what similar advisers charge**, and any unusual fee must be explained.
- **Misuse of the term "fee-only."** An adviser or representative who receives **any** commission, trail, 12b-1 payment, referral fee, or insurance override may **not** describe the practice as fee-only. Fee-**based** means fees plus commissions and must be disclosed as such.
- **Misrepresenting qualifications, credentials, services, or the nature of the relationship** — inflating experience, claiming a designation not held or not current, implying regulatory endorsement, or implying that registration is a mark of approval. **No adviser may state or imply that registration means the Administrator has approved the adviser or the adviser's abilities.**
- **Misuse of professional designations** — using a designation that has no meaningful curriculum or examination, or a "senior specialist" credential that suggests expertise the holder does not have. Many states restrict senior-focused designations specifically.

**Where the misrepresentation line falls.** A statement that is literally true can still misrepresent if it creates a false impression. "I am registered with the state securities Administrator" is true. "I am state-approved" implies an endorsement registration never confers, and the model rule bars stating **or implying** that registration means approval of the adviser or of the adviser's abilities. The same logic governs credentials: a designation the person genuinely earned is still misused if it has **lapsed**, or if it is presented as conferring expertise it does not require. The safe formulation names the issuing body and what earning the credential involved.

## Trading practices that harm clients

- **Front-running** — trading the adviser's or an employee's own account ahead of a client order the adviser knows is coming.
- **Cherry-picking / improper trade allocation** — placing block trades and allocating the profitable fills to favored or proprietary accounts and the losers to others. A **written, pre-trade allocation policy applied consistently** is the defense.
- **Scalping** — recommending a security to clients while holding a position the adviser plans to sell into the resulting demand, without disclosure.
- **Principal transactions** — an adviser trading for its **own account** with a client must disclose the capacity **in writing** and obtain the **client's consent before the completion of the transaction** (that is, before settlement, on a trade-by-trade basis). **Blanket prospective consent does not satisfy the rule.**
- **Agency cross transactions** — the adviser acts as broker for both the advisory client and the other party. Permitted only with written client consent obtained in advance (revocable at any time), trade-by-trade written confirmation disclosing the capacity and remuneration, an **annual statement of the number of such transactions and total commissions**, and only where the adviser did not recommend the transaction to **both** sides.

## Outside activity and firm conduct

- **Selling away** — a representative effecting securities transactions outside the scope of the employing firm without the firm's knowledge and written consent.
- **Undisclosed outside business activities** — any other employment, directorship, or compensated activity must be disclosed to the firm and, where material, to clients on **Form ADV Part 2B**.
- **Failing to act on client instructions**, or unreasonably delaying execution of a client's order or withdrawal request.
- **Failing to supervise** — a firm and its supervisors are liable for a representative's misconduct where reasonable supervisory systems and follow-up were absent.
- **Failing to bring the required books and records into compliance**, destroying records, or falsifying them.

**Where the supervisory line falls.** Failure to supervise is not strict liability for whatever an employee does. It is liability for the **absence of a reasonable system**, or for **failing to follow up on a red flag the system produced**. A firm with written procedures, genuine review of correspondence and trading, and documented escalation is generally not liable for a rogue act it could not reasonably have detected. A firm whose exception report flagged an account traded thirty times in a month, and whose supervisor initialed it without inquiry, is liable — the system worked and the human did not.

## Worked scenarios

### The profitable trade nobody authorized

Tomas Herrera, an IAR, manages a $400,000 account for a client who told him verbally to "do whatever you think best." He begins trading on that oral authority on March 3, rebalancing, trimming an overweighted position and buying three funds squarely consistent with the client's stated objective. The account outperforms, the client is delighted, and she signs the written discretionary authorization on May 9.

Every trade was suitable and the client is happy, and Tomas has still violated the rule. An investment adviser may act on **oral authority**, but only for **10 business days after the first discretionary transaction** while the writing is obtained. Tomas took more than two months. What makes this worth studying is everything that turns out to be irrelevant: profit, suitability, client satisfaction, and eventual ratification. The violation is the **absence of the required authority during the period the trades were placed**, and nothing downstream repairs it.

Two near cases. Had the signed authorization arrived on March 14, every trade would be clean. And had the client instead said "buy me 500 shares of the index fund today, you pick when and at what price," that is **time-and-price** discretion — she chose action, asset and amount — needing no written authority at all, though it expires at the end of that day. Change one more fact: if Tomas were a **broker-dealer agent** rather than an IAR, there would be no grace period, and the very first trade on March 3 would be the violation.

### The fee-only practice that was not

Priya Raman advertises her firm as "fee-only." She charges 1% of assets and accepts no securities commissions. She also holds an insurance license, and last year received a $9,000 first-year override on a long-term care policy sold to an advisory client. She disclosed the commission in her brochure, and the client understood it.

The disclosure cures one problem and not the other. Because the compensation was disclosed, there is no undisclosed-compensation violation. But **"fee-only" means no commissions, trails, referral fees, or transaction-based compensation from any source**, insurance overrides included; the accurate label is **fee-based**. This is the cleanest illustration of a practice that is **unethical without being fraudulent** — no client lost money, the conflict was disclosed, and the sole defect is a label that misdescribes how the adviser is paid. It remains grounds for administrative action, because prospective clients rely on exactly that label to compare firms.

### The block trade allocated after the close

Delacroix Partners places one block order each morning and allocates the fills among client accounts and the firm's own account **after the market closes**, once the day's outcome is known. Over a year the proprietary account's average entry price is measurably better than clients' on the same days. Delacroix has no written allocation policy but says allocations are "made fairly, based on account needs."

This is **cherry-picking**, and the defect is structural rather than intentional: allocating after the outcome is known creates the ability to assign winners to favored accounts, and the statistical pattern across a year is the evidence regulators actually use. Good intentions are not a defense; a **written, pre-trade allocation methodology applied consistently** is — typically pro rata by order size, with documented exceptions. The near case that comes out the other way: a firm that fixes its allocation when the order is entered may still fill accounts unevenly for legitimate reasons, such as a minimum viable position size or a tax-sensitive account, provided the written policy says so **in advance**.

## The mental model

When a question describes conduct and asks whether it is permitted, run three tests in order:

1. **Was it disclosed** in a way a reasonable client would understand, and where required, consented to?
2. **Was the client's interest placed first**, or did the adviser's economic interest drive the recommendation?
3. **Was the required authority in place** — written discretion, written consent, firm approval — *before* the act?

A "no" to any of the three usually makes the answer a violation.

## The questions this topic is tested with

**A client telephones her agent and says, "Buy 500 shares of XYZ for me today — you pick the moment and work the price." The agent has no written trading authorization. The agent executes later that afternoon. This is:**

When the client has specified the security, the amount, and whether to buy or sell, an agent may use judgment as to time and price for that day without written discretionary authority. Anything beyond time and price — choosing the security, the size, or the side — requires prior written authorization.

**An investment adviser representative is short of cash and borrows $10,000 from a longtime advisory client who is a retired schoolteacher, signing a promissory note at a market rate of interest. This practice is:**

NASAA prohibits an adviser or its representatives from borrowing money or securities from a client unless the client is a broker-dealer, an affiliate of the adviser, or a financial institution in the business of lending. A retired teacher fits none of those exceptions, and consent or a market interest rate does not cure the violation.

**Which of the following statements about guaranteeing performance is accurate?**

The prohibition targets a broker-dealer, agent, or adviser promising to absorb client losses, which distorts the risk the client actually bears. Describing a genuine third-party guarantee of principal, interest, or dividends is factual disclosure, not a prohibited guarantee, though the guarantee does not eliminate market risk on resale.

**An agent with discretionary authority over a conservative retiree account executes 140 trades in six months, generating commissions equal to 9% of the account value, with an average holding period of eleven days. The most likely violation is:**

Churning is excessive trading in light of the client resources, objectives, and account character, undertaken to generate commissions rather than to serve the client. High turnover, a short holding period, and a large cost-to-equity ratio in a conservative account are the classic indicators.

**An investment adviser learns that a large institutional client will place a block buy order in a thinly traded stock at the open. The adviser buys the stock for its own account fifteen minutes before entering the client order. This is best described as:**

Front-running is trading for one's own account ahead of a known customer order that is likely to move the market price. Cherry-picking is a different abuse — allocating profitable fills to favored or proprietary accounts after the fact.

**An adviser places block trades each morning and does not assign the fills to specific accounts until after the close, routing the best-performing fills to the accounts of its principals and the worst to advisory clients. This practice is:**

Delaying allocation so the adviser can steer winning trades to itself is cherry-picking, a breach of fiduciary duty and a fraudulent practice under §206. The cure is a written allocation policy applied before trades are placed, with pro rata allocation among participating accounts.
