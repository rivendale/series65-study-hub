# Investment Adviser and IAR Registration

This is one topic from the Series 65 exam, the Uniform Investment Adviser Law
Examination. It is worth about 4 percent of the exam.

## Why this topic matters

Whether a person must register as an investment adviser turns on a three-prong test, a short list of exclusions, and then a jurisdictional question: SEC or state. This topic covers the three prongs, the exclusions candidates most often misapply, the $110M federal covered threshold and its buffer band, why IAR registration is always a state matter, and the Form ADV filing and brochure-delivery mechanics.

## Why There Is a Registration Requirement at All

Investment advice is an unusual product: the buyer pays before being able to judge it, and often cannot judge it afterward, since a bad recommendation and an unlucky good one look identical. The adviser usually holds discretion, access, or influence over the client's money — a relationship of trust, not arm's-length bargaining. Registration answers that asymmetry three ways: it puts a name and a disciplinary history on file *before* solicitation; it forces conflicts into standard, comparable disclosure; and it creates **jurisdiction** — a registered person can be examined, subpoenaed, suspended, and barred.

That last purpose explains the act's reach. The **Uniform Securities Act** covers conduct that **originates in, is directed into, or is accepted in** a state, so an adviser who telephones a prospect across a state line has acted in both. "I have no office there" is rarely a complete defense.

## The Three-Prong Test

Under Section 202(a)(11) of the **Investment Advisers Act of 1940** and the parallel definition in the **Uniform Securities Act**, a person is an investment adviser if all three of the following are true:

1. **Advice about securities.** The advice must concern securities — specific recommendations, asset allocation among securities categories, or the selection and monitoring of other advisers. Advice limited to rare coins, real estate, commodities futures, life insurance without a securities component, or general economic conditions is not securities advice.
2. **Compensation.** Any economic benefit counts. It need not be a separate advisory fee, need not come from the client, and need not be labeled a fee. A commission, a markup, a subscription price, or a bundled charge all satisfy the prong.
3. **In the business.** The advisory activity must be regular, not isolated. Holding oneself out as an adviser, receiving separate compensation for advice, or giving specific securities advice with any frequency establishes this prong.

**All three must be present.** Remove any one and the person falls outside the definition. A friend who suggests a stock over dinner fails the compensation and business prongs. A newsletter that sells subscriptions but writes only about macroeconomics fails the securities prong.

Each prong is drawn wide because each is where evasion would otherwise happen. The **securities prong** reaches advice about *whether* to be in securities at all: moving a client from a bank CD into equities is securities advice though no ticker is named. The **compensation prong** defeats the label game — if it required a line item called "advisory fee," any firm could rename it and vanish from the statute, so the standard is any economic benefit, direct or indirect, including third-party payments the client never sees. The **business prong** is satisfied most easily by **holding out** — a card, a website, a title.

### Hard Case: The Planner Who Never Names a Security

Dana is a fee-only planner charging a flat $3,500 for a comprehensive plan covering budgeting, insurance, college funding, and retirement. She insists she never recommends a specific security; implementation goes to a broker.

Dana is still an investment adviser. Her plan tells the client to hold roughly 60% of retirement assets in equity funds and 40% in bond funds — advice about securities as a class satisfies prong one, the $3,500 is compensation, and she holds out and does this for a living.

Change one fact. If her plans address only cash flow, debt paydown, and term life, and expressly decline to address investments, the securities prong fails and she is not an investment adviser, though she may be regulated as an insurance producer. **The boundary is the subject matter of the advice, not its specificity.**

### Hard Case: The Newsletter That Turns Personalized

Publishers of **bona fide** newspapers, newsletters, magazines, or business publications of **general and regular circulation** are excluded. The Supreme Court in *Lowe v. SEC* framed this around whether the publication is genuinely a publication rather than personalized advice in disguise: content must be **impersonal**, publication must be **regular** (not timed to market events), and it must be **bona fide** (real content, not a promotional vehicle). The rationale is partly constitutional — writing about markets for the public is speech — and partly structural: once the writer answers *this* subscriber's holdings, the trust asymmetry appears.

Ridgeline Letter publishes every second Tuesday to 4,000 subscribers and discusses one model portfolio for everyone. Excluded. Ridgeline then adds a premium tier: for $1,200 more, a subscriber emails her holdings and receives written comments on her allocation. **That tier is advisory business and pulls Ridgeline into the definition.**

The other failure mode is irregularity: a "newsletter" issued only when the author has something to promote — three issues in a week around a small-cap he owns, then silence — is neither regular nor bona fide, which is exactly the pump-scheme pattern the regularity test screens for.

### Hard Case: The CPA Whose Fee Structure Changes

**L**awyers, **A**ccountants, **T**eachers, and **E**ngineers are excluded when the advice is **solely incidental** to the practice of their profession **and** carries **no special compensation**. Both conditions must hold. The rationale: these professions are already licensed and supervised, and some investment-adjacent commentary is unavoidable in tax, estate, and litigation work. The exclusion covers that overlap — not a second business built inside the first.

Marcus is a CPA. Year one he prepares 300 returns at hourly rates and, in the course of that work, tells clients which fund positions are throwing off unwanted capital gain distributions. Solely incidental, no special compensation — **excluded**.

Year two he adds a $2,000 annual "portfolio review," advertised on the firm website. The fee buys advice itself — **special compensation** — and the service is offered in its own right, so it is not solely incidental. **Both conditions fail; Marcus must register.**

Year three he drops the separate fee, raises his hourly rate for clients wanting portfolio commentary, and lists "investment consulting" as a service line. Still not saved: special compensation asks whether a charge is attributable to advice, not whether it sits on its own invoice, and the service line is holding out.

## Exclusion Is Not Exemption

- An **exclusion** means the person **is not an investment adviser** under the definition at all; the adviser provisions never reached them.
- An **exemption** means the person **is** an adviser but is **relieved of the duty to register**. Antifraud still applies, the Administrator still has jurisdiction, and reporting duties may continue.

Test by consequence, not label. "Not required to register but still subject to the antifraud provisions" is an exemption. "Is not an investment adviser" is an exclusion.

## Exclusions from the IA Definition, One at a Time

### Banks

A bank or savings institution is excluded because it is already examined for safety and soundness by banking authorities, and Congress declined to layer a second regime on top. A **bank holding company is not a bank** — a favorite trap. It takes no deposits and is not examined as a depository, so the justification does not reach it; if it gives advisory services, it registers.

### Broker-Dealers and the Wrap Fee

A broker-dealer or its registered representative is excluded when advice is **solely incidental** to brokerage and there is **no special compensation**. Commissions are the normal compensation of a brokerage business, so commissions alone do not destroy the exclusion — a broker cannot execute intelligently without saying something about a trade.

A **wrap fee account** does destroy it. A wrap fee is one asset-based charge covering advice, execution, and custody; part of it unmistakably buys advice, so the firm has special compensation and must register. A rep recommending a fund and earning a sales load stays inside the exclusion; the same rep charging $500 for a written plan is outside it. Ongoing **discretion** is another edge — under current SEC interpretation, discretionary management is generally not merely incidental to brokerage.

### Government Securities Advisers

A person whose advice relates **only** to securities issued or guaranteed by the United States government is excluded: Treasury and agency obligations carry no issuer-credit story worth policing and no promoter with an incentive to mislead. The word doing the work is **only**. Add corporate bonds — or municipals, which are exempt *securities* but not US government obligations — and the exclusion is gone.

## Exemptions from Registration

An adviser who *is* an investment adviser may still avoid registering in a given state. Under the model act: **no place of business in the state and only institutional clients**; or the **state de minimis exemption** — no place of business and **five or fewer** non-institutional clients in the state in the preceding 12 months. Section 222 of the Advisers Act sets de minimis as a national floor.

De minimis solved a real problem. Without it, an adviser whose long-standing client retired and moved across a state line faced full registration — filings, fees, exams — for one client and no operations. Across a mobile population that meant a compliance bill out of all proportion to the business, or the ugly alternative of firing loyal clients. The band lets the incidental relationship continue while making clear that a real book of business, or any physical presence, requires registration. **That rationale is why a place of business defeats the exemption at one client: an office is never incidental.**

## Federal Covered vs State Registration

**NSMIA** divided the world between SEC and state registration; an adviser generally registers in one place, not both. Before 1996 a national adviser filed with the SEC *and* with every state it touched, duplicating review for little added protection. NSMIA drew a size line: large advisers, whose reach is national, answer to the national regulator; small advisers, whose harm is local and who are examined more efficiently locally, answer to the states.

- Adviser: $110M or more in regulatory AUM. Registers with: SEC (mandatory).
- Adviser: $100M to $110M. Registers with: Optional — the buffer band; may stay state-registered.
- Adviser: Under $100M. Registers with: State(s).
- Adviser: Adviser to a registered investment company. Registers with: SEC, at any AUM.
- Adviser: Required to register in 15 or more states. Registers with: SEC (multi-state exemption).
- Adviser: Adviser with no state registration requirement in its home state. Registers with: SEC.


The **buffer band** exists so advisers do not switch regulators every time markets move. Without it, a firm near the line would withdraw federally and register in six states after a bad quarter, then reverse the exercise after a good one. It is measured at the **annual updating amendment**, not continuously, for the same reason.

### Crossing in Each Direction

The directions are not symmetric, and the exam likes that. **Upward:** a state-registered adviser reporting **$110M or more** at its annual updating amendment **must** apply to the SEC, then withdraw its state registrations once federal registration is effective; between $100M and $110M it **may** register federally but is not compelled to. **Downward:** an SEC-registered adviser reporting AUM **below $90M** at its annual amendment is no longer eligible and must withdraw federally and register with the states. Eligibility to *stay* SEC-registered therefore runs down to $90M — a second buffer beneath the first — with a transition window to complete the state filings.

### Worked Scenario: Meridian Advisors Crosses the Line

Meridian is registered in three states. At its annual updating amendment it reports regulatory AUM of $104M, up from $88M on market appreciation alone. It is in the **buffer band**: it may register with the SEC but need not, and staying state-registered is usually cheaper.

The next year it reports $121M. Over $110M, SEC registration is **mandatory**. It registers federally, then files Form ADV-W in the three states — but each may still require a **notice filing**, so the state relationship does not disappear, it changes character.

Two years later, after redemptions, it reports $86M. Below $90M it is no longer eligible federally, so it withdraws and registers where it has a place of business or more than a de minimis client count. At $95M it would have done nothing at all — the point of the band.

**Federal covered advisers** are not free of the states. A state may require a **notice filing** — Form ADV, a consent to service of process, and a fee — and keeps full **antifraud** authority. The state loses the power to review the adviser and impose substantive requirements; it keeps the power to know who is operating there, to be served, and to prosecute fraud.

### Private Fund and Venture Capital Adviser Exemptions

An adviser solely to **private funds** with less than **$150M** in US assets under management is exempt from SEC registration but remains an **exempt reporting adviser** filing portions of Form ADV. An adviser solely to **venture capital funds** is exempt at any size, also as an ERA. Exempt does not mean unregulated — antifraud, books and records, and reporting continue. The logic is sophistication plus visibility: these investors can negotiate for themselves, but after 2008 regulators were unwilling to be blind to the sector.

## Investment Adviser Representatives

An **IAR** is a natural person associated with an investment adviser who makes recommendations, manages accounts, determines recommendations, solicits advisory services, or supervises those who do. Read it as five functions and register anyone performing any one:

- **Makes or gives** advice to clients.
- **Manages accounts or portfolios**, including making the decisions.
- **Determines** which recommendations will be given — the analyst who sets the model portfolio is an IAR though he never speaks to a client.
- **Solicits or negotiates** the sale of advisory services — the salesperson is an IAR even if she never gives advice.
- **Supervises** any of the above.

Not IARs: the receptionist, operations staff processing transfers, IT and marketing personnel who never discuss advisory services with prospects, the firm's bookkeeper. The line is functional, not hierarchical — an owner who only runs the business may fall outside it while a junior analyst setting model weights falls inside. At a nine-person firm, the two owners who manage portfolios, the analyst who builds the models, the business-development officer who signs clients up, and the compliance officer who approves recommendations all register; the client-service associate who only reports balances does not — until the day she answers "should I add to this position."

**IAR registration is always at the state level — even for IARs of federal covered advisers.** The SEC registers firms, not individuals. This follows from NSMIA's logic: preemption eliminated duplicative *entity* review, while individual licensing and discipline stayed where the states had always run it.

**IAR de minimis:** an IAR of a federal covered adviser must register in a state only if the IAR has a **place of business** there. Under the state model, an IAR with **no place of business** in a state and **fewer than 6 retail clients** there in the preceding 12 months need not register. A **place of business defeats de minimis instantly**.

## Form ADV and What Each Part Is For

Filings go through the **IARD** system. The four pieces are not redundant; each answers a different question for a different reader.

- **Part 1** — check-the-box regulatory data: ownership, AUM, disciplinary history, business practices. **Its reader is the regulator.** It is structured data so it can be searched, compared across firms, and used to target examinations.
- **Part 2A** — the **firm brochure**, plain English and narrative: services, fees, conflicts, disciplinary events. **Its reader is the client.** Plain English is substantive — a brochure that buries a revenue-sharing conflict in defined terms has not disclosed it. Part 2A is where the fiduciary duty to disclose conflicts is operationalized.
- **Part 2B** — the **brochure supplement** on the individuals who advise the client: education, experience, discipline, other business. Part 2A describes the firm; **2B describes the person handling the account.**
- **Part 3 (Form CRS)** — a short **relationship summary** for retail investors, delivered at or before the start of the relationship. Its format is prescribed and its length capped because its purpose is *comparison*: an investor should be able to lay a broker-dealer's version beside an adviser's.

### Brochure Delivery Rule

Scope the rule before you time it, because the two regimes are not the same. Under the **NASAA model brochure rule**, which governs **state-registered** advisers, deliver Part 2A either **at least 48 hours before** entering the advisory contract, **or at the time** of entering it, in which case the client has **5 business days to rescind without penalty**. That structure is a choice between time to read and a right to change your mind. The **federal** rule is different: **Advisers Act Rule 204-3**, as amended in 2010, requires an SEC-registered adviser to deliver **before or at the time** of entering the contract, with **no** 48-hour alternative and **no** rescission window. The 48-hour/5-business-day construct mirrors the pre-2011 federal rule and now survives only at the state level, so read the fact pattern for which regulator is in play. Both regimes then require **annual** delivery, within **120 days of the adviser's fiscal year end**, of either the updated brochure or a summary of material changes with an offer to provide the full document.

### Amendments

- **Annual updating amendment** within **90 days** of fiscal year end.
- **Prompt (other-than-annual) amendment** whenever information becomes **materially inaccurate** — especially disciplinary events, custody, or contact information.

The two annual deadlines are swapped constantly. Sequence them: the firm tells the **regulator** first (90 days), then the **client** (120 days).

## The Registration Lifecycle

**Filing.** Form ADV through IARD, with the consent to service of process, the fee, and any state-required financial statements. The consent lets the Administrator be served on the applicant's behalf.

**Effectiveness.** Under the model act, registration becomes effective at **noon on the thirtieth day** after a complete application is filed if no denial order or proceeding is pending; the Administrator may set an earlier date. The waiting period gives the Administrator a window to review, and to act if necessary, before the applicant can solicit anyone.

**Renewal.** State registrations of advisers, IARs, broker-dealers, and agents **expire December 31** and are renewed annually with a fee. Renewal is not automatic; a lapsed registration leaves the person unregistered, and continuing to act is a violation, not a late filing.

**Withdrawal.** An adviser withdraws on **Form ADV-W**, and two things matter more than any date. **Withdrawal is not immediate** — it takes effect after a waiting period following filing, and the two regimes use different periods. A state registration withdrawn under the **Uniform Securities Act** becomes effective **30 days** after filing, and the Administrator may set an earlier date; adopting states can and do vary the figure, so a fact pattern naming a state statute controls over the model number. A federal covered adviser withdrawing on ADV-W is governed by **SEC Rule 203-2**, under which the withdrawal becomes effective **60 days** after filing. And **a pending proceeding suspends it**: filing ADV-W does not let a registrant walk away from an instituted revocation or denial proceeding.

**Retained jurisdiction.** Even after withdrawal takes effect, the Administrator generally **retains jurisdiction for one year** to institute proceedings over conduct occurring while the person was registered. Without that, resignation would be a complete defense and revocation worthless against anyone willing to quit first.

## Net Worth and Bonding

States, not the SEC, set financial requirements for state-registered advisers, and the amounts **vary by state** — so learn the structure, not a national figure. The **NASAA model rule** pattern, adopted with variations, tiers the requirement by the risk the business creates: an adviser with **custody** carries the highest minimum net worth because client assets are physically at risk; an adviser with **discretionary authority** but no custody carries a lower minimum, since it can trade but cannot abscond; and an adviser accepting **substantial prepayment of fees** well in advance of service must at minimum maintain a **positive net worth**, so clients are not prepaying an insolvent firm.

A state may permit a **surety bond** in place of net worth, and may require one of an adviser with discretion or custody; the bond runs for the benefit of clients the adviser harms.

The **NASAA model rule (202(d)-1)** puts numbers on that structure, and adopting states start from them even where they vary: **$35,000** minimum net worth with **custody**, **$10,000** with **discretion** but no custody, and merely a **positive** net worth — not negative — for an adviser accepting prepayment of **more than $500 per client six or more months in advance**. Hold these as the shape of the tiering rather than as a national standard, because your own state's figures govern.

**The notice obligation is the part candidates skip.** An adviser whose net worth falls below the minimum must **notify the Administrator by the close of the next business day**, and then file a **detailed financial report by the close of business on the business day after that**. Two deadlines one day apart, and the duty is **self-reporting** — nobody comes asking first.

One preemption point completes the picture. Under **Section 222 of the Advisers Act**, a state may not impose net worth or bonding requirements on an adviser whose **principal place of business is in another state**, so long as the adviser complies with its home state's requirements. An adviser registered in four states meets one net worth standard, not four.

## IAR Continuing Education

The **NASAA Model Rule on IAR continuing education** requires **12 credits annually**: **6 in Products and Practice** and **6 in Ethics and Professional Responsibility**. It has been adopted by a growing majority of states; where adopted, an IAR who fails to complete CE becomes **CE inactive** and cannot renew until the deficiency is cured. Credits do not carry over — an IAR who takes 24 credits in one year has banked nothing for the next — and an IAR registered in several states satisfies the requirement once through the model rule's reciprocity.

## The questions this topic is tested with

**Under the Uniform Securities Act, a person falls within the definition of investment adviser only if the person:**

The three-prong test requires all three elements together: advice about securities, provided as part of a regular business, for compensation. Failing any single prong takes the person outside the definition entirely.

**Delia is a certified public accountant. While preparing a tax return she tells the client that municipal bond interest would lower his tax burden and suggests shifting part of his taxable bond holdings into municipals. She bills only her ordinary hourly tax-preparation rate. Under the Uniform Securities Act, Delia is:**

Lawyers, accountants, teachers and engineers (the LATE group) are excluded when securities advice is solely incidental to the practice of the profession and no special compensation is charged for it. Note that this is an exclusion from the definition, not an exemption from registration — if she began charging a separate advisory fee, the exclusion would evaporate.

**Harbor Point Securities is a registered broker-dealer. It launches a wrap-fee program under which clients pay a single annual charge of 1.25% of assets covering advice, portfolio management and all trade executions. With respect to the wrap program, Harbor Point is:**

The broker-dealer exclusion applies only where advisory services are solely incidental to the brokerage business and no special compensation is received. A wrap fee is a single charge that plainly covers advice, so it is special compensation and the firm must register as an investment adviser for that program.

**Which of the following is NOT excluded from the definition of investment adviser under the Uniform Securities Act?**

Banks, savings institutions and trust companies are excluded from the investment adviser definition. A bank holding company is a separate corporate entity that is not itself a bank, so it receives no exclusion — one of the most frequently tested distinctions on the exam.

**Crestline Advisers reports regulatory assets under management of $104 million on its annual updating amendment. It maintains a place of business in a single state. Which statement is correct?**

SEC registration becomes mandatory at $110 million or more in regulatory assets under management. Between $100 million and $110 million the adviser may elect either regime, which spares firms hovering near the line from switching regulators every year.

**Ridgeway Capital manages $38 million and serves as investment adviser to a registered open-end investment company. Ridgeway must:**

An adviser to a registered investment company must register with the SEC without regard to any dollar threshold. The assets-under-management tests simply do not apply to that category of adviser.
